For a planet on a circular orbit, the probability that it transits its host star is:

$$P_{\rm tr} \approx \frac{R_\star}{a}$$

where $a$ is the orbital separation (the $\sqrt{1-e^2}$ factor is 1 for circular orbits).

## Finding the orbital separations

From Kepler's third law, $P^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G M_\star}$, so:

$$a \propto M_\star^{1/3} P^{2/3}$$

**Ratio of semi-major axes:**
$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right)^{2/3} = 2^{1/3}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{2/3} = \left(\frac{2}{9}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.606$$

So Planet_1 orbits much more tightly ($a_2/a_1 \approx 1.65$).

## Ratio of transit probabilities

Since both host stars have the **same radius**, $R_1 = R_2$:

$$\frac{P_{{\rm tr},1}}{P_{{\rm tr},2}} = \frac{R_1/a_1}{R_2/a_2} = \frac{a_2}{a_1} = \left(\frac{9}{2}\right)^{1/3} \approx 1.65$$

So Planet_1 is ~1.65 times more likely to transit (it sits closer to its star, which is more massive but has the same radius).
